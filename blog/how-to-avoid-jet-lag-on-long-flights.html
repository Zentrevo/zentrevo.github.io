How to Avoid Jet Lag on Long Flights

What actually works to reset your body clock faster — before, during, and after a long-haul flight.

Jet lag happens because your body's internal clock, the circadian rhythm, doesn't adjust to a new time zone as fast as a plane can get you there. The good news: some of the fix is genuinely within your control, and it starts before you even board.

Before the Flight

Shift Your Sleep Schedule a Few Days Early

If you're heading somewhere several time zones ahead, start shifting your bedtime and wake time by 30-60 minutes per day, 2-3 days before departure. It's a small adjustment that gives your body a head start instead of asking it to jump the entire gap in one go.

Pick Flight Times Strategically

Where you have a choice, flights that arrive in the early evening local time (rather than early morning) tend to make the first night's sleep easier to time correctly, since you're not forcing yourself to stay awake for a full extra day.

During the Flight

Set Your Watch to Destination Time Immediately

As soon as you board, mentally switch to destination time. Sleep, eat, and stay awake according to that clock rather than your departure time zone — it sounds simple, but it meaningfully speeds up the mental adjustment.

Manage Light Exposure

Light is the single strongest signal your circadian rhythm responds to. Use an eye mask to block light when it's "night" at your destination, even if it's daytime where you're flying, and seek out light when it should be morning there.

Limit Alcohol and Caffeine

Both disrupt sleep quality more than they help on a long flight. Alcohol in particular feels like it helps you fall asleep but actually fragments sleep and worsens dehydration, which compounds jet lag symptoms.

Jet Lag Recovery Checklist

  • Hydrate consistently throughout the flight — cabin air is extremely dry and dehydration makes jet lag worse
  • Get outside in natural daylight as soon as possible after landing, especially if it's daytime at your destination
  • Avoid a long nap on arrival day if it's daytime — a 20-minute power nap is fine, a 3-hour one resets nothing
  • Stay on destination meal times from your first full day, even if your appetite hasn't caught up yet
  • Give yourself 1 day of adjustment per time zone crossed as a rough recovery estimate

"Jet lag isn't something that happens to you. It's something your schedule negotiates with your body — and you can tip the negotiation."

Does Direction of Travel Matter?

Yes — flying east (losing time) is consistently harder on the body than flying west (gaining time), since it's easier for most people's circadian rhythm to stretch a day longer than to compress it shorter. If you're planning a long international relocation or extended trip, factor in a buffer day or two after eastbound long-haul flights specifically, rather than diving straight into a packed itinerary.

None of this eliminates jet lag entirely on genuinely long routes, but a deliberate pre-flight and in-flight routine consistently cuts recovery time compared to just winging it and hoping for the best.

Frequently Asked Questions

How many days does jet lag actually last?

A common rule of thumb is about one day of recovery per time zone crossed, though this varies by person and by direction of travel.

Is eastbound or westbound travel worse for jet lag?

Eastbound travel is generally harder, since it requires shortening your day, while westbound travel lets you extend it — which most people's bodies handle more easily.

Do melatonin supplements help with jet lag?

Many travelers find them helpful for adjusting sleep timing, though it's worth discussing timing and dosage with a doctor before your trip rather than experimenting for the first time mid-journey.

Should I sleep on a long flight or stay awake?

Base it on your destination's local time, not your departure time zone — sleep if it will be nighttime when you land, stay awake if it will be daytime.
