Istanbul's skyline of domes and minarets bridges Europe and Asia, while Cappadocia's fairy chimneys drift past hundreds of sunrise hot-air balloons.

Turkey is a country that sits, quite literally, between two worlds — the European and Asian continents meet across the Bosphorus Strait in Istanbul, and that sense of crossroads runs through everything the country offers. It is one of the most historically layered destinations on earth, having been home to the Byzantine and Ottoman Empires, and its cities carry the fingerprints of both.

Istanbul is usually where a Turkey trip begins. The Hagia Sophia, once a Byzantine cathedral and later an Ottoman mosque, sits almost across the square from the Blue Mosque with its six soaring minarets and cascading domes. Nearby, Topkapi Palace holds centuries of royal history, while the Grand Bazaar — one of the oldest and largest covered markets in the world — is a maze of lantern shops, spice stalls, and hand-woven carpets. A sunset cruise along the Bosphorus, with the call to prayer drifting over the water and the skyline lit gold, is one of those travel moments people remember for years.

Beyond Istanbul, Cappadocia is Turkey's other unmissable icon. Its surreal landscape of "fairy chimney" rock formations was shaped over millions of years by volcanic ash and erosion, and centuries ago, entire underground cities and cave churches were carved into the soft stone. Today, many of those cave dwellings have been converted into boutique hotels, and the region is famous worldwide for its sunrise hot-air balloon flights — hundreds of balloons drifting silently over the valleys is a sight that rarely fails to move even well-travelled visitors.

The Turkish coastline adds an entirely different mood to the trip. Along the Aegean and Mediterranean, towns like Bodrum, Fethiye, and Antalya offer turquoise water, relaxed beach clubs, and ancient ruins scattered along the shoreline — including Ephesus, one of the best-preserved classical cities anywhere in the world, where marble streets once walked by Romans are still intact enough to wander today.

Food is central to any Turkish itinerary. Expect slow-cooked kebabs and köfte, warm flatbreads straight from a clay oven, mezze spreads of smoky eggplant and yoghurt-based dips, and Turkish tea served constantly, in small tulip-shaped glasses, wherever you go. No trip is complete without baklava from a proper pastry shop, and a strong, unfiltered Turkish coffee at the end of a meal — traditionally followed by someone offering to read your fortune from the grounds left in the cup.

Turkey is also a genuinely practical destination for travellers: it's visa-friendly for many nationalities, well connected by direct flights, has a mature tourism infrastructure, and offers a wide range of experiences from five-star resorts to family-run guesthouses, so it suits everyone from first-time international travellers to seasoned explorers looking for something with real depth.

The best time to visit depends on what you're after: spring (April to May) and early autumn (September to October) bring mild weather that's ideal for both Istanbul sightseeing and Cappadocia's balloon season, while summer suits the coast if a beach holiday is the priority. Winter, though quieter, has its own charm — Istanbul's mosques and bazaars are far less crowded, and snow occasionally dusts Cappadocia's rock formations for a strikingly different look.

Getting around is straightforward with a bit of planning: Istanbul's tram and ferry network makes the historic core easy to navigate on foot and by water, while domestic flights connect the city to Cappadocia and the coast in under an hour and a half.
